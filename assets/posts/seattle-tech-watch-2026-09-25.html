Seattle Tech Watch: Row Zero, Hubble Network and the Next Wave of AI Agents

September 25, 2026 • DJ The PC Dude • Seattle, Washington

Three Seattle-area stories from this week point in the same direction: the region is pushing deeper into cloud-scale data, connected hardware and specialized AI systems that do real work instead of simply chatting.

Databricks buys Seattle spreadsheet startup Row Zero

Databricks acquired Seattle startup Row Zero, whose spreadsheet software was designed to work with very large enterprise datasets. Databricks says it plans to bring Row Zero into its data and AI platform so non-technical teams can work with large datasets through a familiar spreadsheet-style interface.

The important part is not the spreadsheet itself. It is the continuing collapse of the wall between traditional office software and cloud-scale data systems. More users are going to expect spreadsheet-like simplicity while working with datasets that used to require a data engineer.

Hubble Network raises $200M for Bluetooth-from-space

Seattle-based Hubble Network raised $200 million at a reported $1.6 billion valuation. Its goal is unusual: allow ordinary Bluetooth devices to communicate through satellites in low-Earth orbit. The company currently operates six satellites and plans a much larger constellation.

If this architecture works at scale, it could affect tracking, industrial sensors, logistics and remote devices that cannot depend on normal cellular coverage. This is another reminder that Seattle tech is not only software anymore; aerospace, networking and edge devices are becoming a larger part of the region's technology identity.

Augmeta gives business metrics their own AI agents

Redmond startup Augmeta raised $3 million to build what it calls agentic KPI operations. Instead of waiting for a weekly business review, the software assigns AI agents to watch specific business metrics and investigate why a number changed.

This is a more realistic AI business model than a generic chatbot. The software is attached to a specific measurable job: watch a metric, detect a change, investigate it and surface useful context.

DJ The PC Dude Take

Seattle's strongest technology pattern right now is specialization. Data tools are becoming easier to use, connected hardware is reaching farther, and AI agents are being tied to narrow business workflows. For independent tech work, that favors practical tools that solve one clear problem well.

What this means for small businesses and PC users

  • Cloud and AI tools will increasingly expect reliable browsers, fast storage, enough memory and strong account security.
  • Specialized automation can be valuable, but businesses should know exactly what data an agent can access.
  • Local technical services can compete by connecting ordinary users to increasingly complicated cloud, networking and AI systems.
  • Tools that explain failures, permissions and data flows may be more useful than another generic AI interface.
